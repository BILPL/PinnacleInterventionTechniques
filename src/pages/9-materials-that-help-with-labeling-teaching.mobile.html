When Your Child Knows But Can't Name
A Parent's Voice
"My son is four and a half, and I know he understands so much more than he can say. When I ask 'Where's the ball?' he looks right at it. When I say 'Get your shoes,' he runs to find them. But when I hold up the same ball and ask 'What is this?' — silence. Or just 'this.' Or sometimes he echoes back 'What is this?'"
"He can identify dozens of objects by pointing, but he can only spontaneously name maybe ten things. And those ten took months of practice. At the park, he sees a dog and gets excited, but he doesn't say 'dog!' — he just pulls my arm and points."

Flashcards bore him after three. Naming everything just makes him tune out. There's a gap between what he knows and what he can say — and these 9 materials help bridge it.

Understanding the Labeling Gap
Labeling (also called "tacting" in Applied Behavior Analysis) is the ability to name objects, actions, properties, or concepts when you see, hear, smell, taste, or touch them. It is a fundamental expressive language skill that develops naturally in typically developing children — but often requires explicit teaching for children with autism or language delays.
Receptive Language ✓
Child can identify objects when named, follow complex instructions ("Put the red block in the big box"), and demonstrate clear understanding of their world.
Expressive Language ✗
Child cannot produce words on demand. Limited spontaneous labeling. Points and gestures effectively but cannot name items when asked "What is this?"

Without labeling skills, children cannot fully express their knowledge, request items by name, describe their experiences, answer questions, or participate in typical conversations. The gap between receptive understanding and expressive output limits communication, education, and social connection.
Common Labeling Challenges
These are the patterns families and therapists see most often — recognizing your child's specific challenge is the first step to choosing the right approach.
Understands but Can't Produce
Child knows words receptively but cannot produce them expressively on demand.
Prompt-Dependent
Labeling only occurs with heavy prompting — never spontaneously in natural contexts.
No Motivation to Name
Pointing and gesturing work effectively — why would the child bother labeling?
Flashcard Boredom
Quick disengagement from traditional repetitive approaches after just a few trials.
Echolalia
Repeating the question ("What is this?") rather than producing the label.
Generalization Failure
Knows "ball" for one red ball but doesn't apply the label to blue balls or different sizes.
The Verbal Behavior Framework
Understanding labeling within verbal behavior theory helps parents and therapists choose the right teaching strategies. A tact is a verbal operant where a word is evoked by a nonverbal stimulus — seeing a ball → saying "Ball!" — and reinforced by social consequences like praise and acknowledgment.
Unlike manding (requesting, driven by motivation for items), tacting is driven purely by the presence of stimuli in the environment. This is why a child who can request "juice!" by pointing may still struggle to label "juice" when shown a picture of it. The two skills require different teaching approaches.
Why Labels Matter So Much
Labeling is foundational for every higher language skill. When children build expressive vocabulary, they unlock a cascade of communication abilities that go far beyond simply naming objects.
Requesting
Children who can label items can also request them by name — moving from pointing at the juice to saying "I want juice."
Describing
Labels expand into descriptions: "The big red ball," "The soft bunny," "The loud truck." Rich vocabulary enables rich expression.
Conversing
Without labels, children cannot answer questions, comment on their environment, or participate in typical conversations with family.
Learning
Classroom participation, reading comprehension, and academic progress all depend on a robust expressive vocabulary foundation.

Real Object Collection Boxes

Material 1 of 9 Touch it. Hold it. Name it. Children learn to label best when they can see, touch, and interact with real objects — not just pictures. Real object collection boxes organize actual items into categories for labeling practice: a box of real fruits, a box of toy vehicles, a box of clothing items, a box of kitchen tools. Real objects have weight, texture, smell, and three-dimensionality that pictures simply lack. A child can hold the real apple, feel its smooth skin, smell it, and connect the word "apple" to the full sensory experience. This multi-sensory association is stronger and more generalizable than picture-only learning. The concrete-to-abstract progression mirrors natural language development. Key Insight: Real objects create multi-sensory word associations that are stronger and more generalizable than picture-only learning. Start with real objects, then transfer to pictures.

Real Object Collection Boxes — DIY Guide
Categories to Start
  • Food/fruits (plastic fruits work for durability)
  • Vehicles (small toy car, bus, train, airplane, boat)
  • Animals (toy animals, realistic figures)
  • Clothing (actual small items: sock, hat, shirt, shoe)
  • Household items (spoon, cup, brush, keys)
  • Body parts (use a doll for practicing)
Organization Tips
  • One clear container per category
  • Start with 5–8 items per category — not overwhelming
  • Photo of contents on outside to help child anticipate
  • Rotate categories to maintain interest
Teaching Progression
  1. Child labels while holding object
  1. Child labels when shown object
  1. Child labels object in natural context (seeing apple in kitchen)
  1. Child labels object in picture
  1. Child labels from memory ("What fruit is red?")
Teaching With Real Objects
  • Present one item at a time
  • Let child hold and explore freely
  • Model label clearly: "Apple. This is an apple."
  • Prompt child to label
  • Reinforce successful labeling enthusiastically
Price Range: ₹500–2,000 (collecting and organizing household items) | Largely DIY/free

Safety Note: Ensure all objects are safe — no small parts for young children, no sharp edges, no choking hazards. Supervise play with real objects at all times.
Material 2 of 9
Errorless Learning Flashcard Sets
Success from the very first try.
Traditional flashcards often lead to errors, frustration, and learned helplessness — the child learns to guess, gets it wrong, feels bad, and stops trying. Errorless learning flashcard sets are designed to maximize success by controlling how cards are presented.
The technique presents one card at a time, immediately models the correct answer, gradually fades prompts, and ensures the child succeeds on nearly every trial. Cards are organized by mastery level: mastered cards for confidence-building, acquiring cards for active teaching, and new cards for introduction. For children who've developed flashcard aversion, errorless learning can restart the teaching process successfully.

Key Insight: Errorless learning builds correct responses from the start, preventing the frustration cycle that kills motivation. Aim for 80%+ correct responses in every session.
Errorless Flashcard Sets — DIY Guide
Card Creation
  • Clear, uncluttered images — one object per card
  • Real photos work better than cartoons for most children
  • White or simple backgrounds reduce distraction
  • Consistent index-card size — laminate for durability
Organization System — 3 Sections
  • MASTERED: Child labels correctly without prompt 3+ times
  • ACQUIRING: Child is learning, needs some prompts
  • NEW: Not yet introduced
Session Structure
  • 2–3 mastered + 1–2 acquiring + 0–1 new cards per session
  • Start with mastered cards for early success
  • If child errors, immediately prompt correct answer — don't dwell
  • Always end the session on a success
Errorless Teaching Procedure
  1. Present the card
  1. Immediately model correct answer (don't wait for error): "Ball!"
  1. Prompt child to repeat: "Say ball."
  1. Child says "Ball."
  1. Reinforce enthusiastically
  1. Gradually fade the prompt over trials
Prompt Fading Sequence
Full model → First sound only → Expectant pause → Child independently labels
Tracking Progress
  • Track mastery on back of card or separate sheet
  • Date when card moves: New → Acquiring → Mastered
  • Review mastered cards periodically to maintain
Price Range: ₹300–800 for commercial sets | Free (homemade)

Safety Note: Keep sessions short and positive. If child shows frustration, end on success with an easy card. Never force through resistance.
Material 3 of 9
Matching and Sorting Trays
Categories before labels.
Before a child can label independently, they often need to understand the relationship between objects and their categories. Matching and sorting trays build this foundation by having children match identical objects, sort objects by category, and match objects to pictures.
A child who sorts all the fruits into one section and all the vehicles into another is demonstrating category knowledge that will directly support category labeling ("What fruit is this?" "What kind of vehicle?"). Matching a real apple to a picture of an apple bridges the real-to-representation gap. Sorting and matching activities are also motivating for many children who resist direct labeling drills — they're engaged without even realizing they're building vocabulary.

Key Insight: Category understanding supports category labeling. Sorting activities build the cognitive framework for vocabulary organization before the labels themselves are required.
Matching and Sorting Trays — DIY Guide
Tray Options
  • Muffin tin (multiple sorting sections)
  • Divided plate or container
  • Ice cube trays for small objects
  • Egg carton sections
  • Plastic divided storage containers
Matching Activities — Start Here
  • Identical matching: Ball to identical ball, cup to identical cup
  • Real-to-real matching first, then real-to-picture
  • 2D-to-2D: Match picture card to identical picture
Sorting Activities — Next Step
  • By category: All fruits together, all animals together
  • By attribute: All red objects, all round objects
  • By function: Things we wear, things we eat
Sorting Progression
  1. Sort identical items (3 apples, 3 balls — put apples together)
  1. Sort within a category but different items (apple, banana, orange all in "fruit")
  1. Sort by attribute across categories (red ball, red apple, red car all in "red")
  1. Sort by function (spoon, fork, plate all in "eating")
Adding Language During Sorting
  • As child sorts, model labels: "Apple! Apple goes with fruits."
  • After sorting: "What's in this section? Fruits!"
  • Request: "Put the banana in the fruits."
  • Eventually: "Where does banana go? What is banana? A fruit!"
Visual Supports
Add picture labels on each section to guide sorting initially. Gradually fade visual supports as child learns categories.
Price Range: ₹200–600

Safety Note: Small sorting objects must be appropriate for the child's developmental level. Supervise closely with objects that could be mouthed or swallowed.
Material 4 of 9
Field-of-Three Presentation Mats
Visual structure for focused learning trials.
When teaching labeling, how you present items matters as much as which items you present. Field-of-three presentation mats create a consistent visual structure for teaching. Three clearly defined spaces hold three objects or pictures. The child learns that when items appear on the mat, a learning trial is happening.
The consistent structure reduces anxiety and increases attention. During teaching, you might present one known item and two unknown, or three items from the same category. The visual clarity of defined spaces helps children focus and reduces the overwhelm of too many choices. Many ABA and speech therapy protocols use field presentations — having consistent mats at home mirrors therapy structure and accelerates learning.

Key Insight: Visual structure reduces cognitive load and creates predictable learning contexts that support attention and memory. The mat signals: learning time.
Field-of-Three Presentation Mats — DIY Guide
Mat Design Specifications
  • Rectangle with three clearly defined sections
  • Each section large enough to hold one object or picture card
  • Sections visually distinct (different colors, clear borders, or raised edges)
  • Size: approximately 30cm × 10cm works well
Material Options
  • Felt or fabric mat (different colored felt squares sewn together)
  • Laminated paper with printed sections
  • Placemat modified with tape sections
  • Small tray with three compartments
Trial Types With the Mat
  • Known/known/unknown: Two mastered items, one new item
  • Same category: Three fruits — "Which one is the banana?"
  • Discrimination: Similar items requiring careful looking
  • Generalization: Three different balls — "They're all… balls!"
Using the Mat
  • Place three items in the three sections
  • Ask labeling question: "What is this?" (pointing to one item)
  • Or receptive: "Touch the apple"
  • Or expressive with choice: "Which one is the ball?"
Progression
  1. Field of 2 for younger children first
  1. Field of 3 (standard)
  1. Larger fields for advanced learners
  1. Receptive on mat → Expressive on mat → Expressive without mat
Consistent Use
Use the same mat across sessions for consistency. The mat signals "structured learning time." Always also practice labeling in natural contexts without the mat to support generalization.
Price Range: ₹100–400 | Free (homemade)

Safety Note: Presentation mats are teaching tools, not rigid requirements. Maintain flexibility and fade structured materials as child progresses.
Material 5 of 9
Motivating Object Sets (High-Preference Items)
Start with what they love. Motivation is everything.
Children are most motivated to label things they care about deeply. Motivating object sets leverage your child's specific interests to drive labeling practice. If your child loves trains, the first fifty labels might all be train-related: train, track, engine, caboose, station, conductor. If they love food, label every food item at every meal. If they love animals, build an animal vocabulary first.
Starting with high-preference items creates intrinsic motivation — the child genuinely wants to talk about these things. Once labeling is established with preferred items, generalization to less interesting items becomes far easier. The principle is elegant: interest drives engagement, engagement drives repetition, repetition drives learning. You don't have to fight for attention when the subject is something your child already loves.

Key Insight: Motivation precedes learning. Build vocabulary in areas of genuine interest first, then systematically expand outward to new categories.
Motivating Object Sets — DIY Guide
Identify Your Child's Preferences
  • What does your child gravitate toward spontaneously?
  • What holds attention longest?
  • What do they request — even non-verbally?
  • What do they look at, play with, talk about already?
Preference-Based Vocabulary Sets
  • Trains: engine, caboose, track, station, bridge, tunnel, conductor, ticket, wheel
  • Food: specific favorites first, then categories (fruit names, vegetable names)
  • Animals: favorites first (dogs? dinosaurs?), then expand
  • Characters: label every character from favorite show
  • Vehicles: if cars are the interest, every type of car
Teaching Strategy
  • Start every session with preferred items
  • Use preferred items as reinforcers for labeling less-preferred items
  • Build massive vocabulary in preferred area first — 50+ labels
  • Then systematically expand to new categories
Interest as a Bridge
"You labeled all the trains! Now let's learn the things at the train station." Expand from a narrow interest to related categories using the preferred topic as a bridge.
Using Interest in Context
Use the interest context creatively: "The train driver is eating… what? Apple!" Connect preferred subjects to non-preferred vocabulary for natural, engaging expansion.
Collect Materials
  • Real objects when possible (toy trains, food items)
  • High-quality pictures of interest items
  • Books focused on interest area
  • Videos that feature interest items (pause to label)
Maintain Motivation — Don't Burn It Out
  • Don't over-drill preferred items — maintain enjoyment
  • Keep some preferred items for pure play (not every interaction is teaching)
  • Watch for signs of reduced interest and rotate materials
Price Range: Varies based on child's interests — most items already at home

Safety Note: Balance structured teaching with pure enjoyment of interests. Don't make every preferred item a labeling trial.
Material 6 of 9
Feature-Function-Class Cards
Beyond "What is it?" — building rich vocabulary depth.
Beyond basic labeling ("What is this?"), children need to understand and express what objects look like (features), what objects do or are used for (functions), and what category objects belong to (class). Feature-Function-Class (FFC) cards teach these deeper labels explicitly.
One side shows the object; the other side lists features (has four legs, is furry, barks), function (guards the house, plays fetch), and class (animal, pet). This expands labeling from simple naming to rich description. A child moves from "dog" to "It's a dog. Dogs are animals. They have fur and four legs. Dogs bark and play fetch." FFC understanding supports answering questions, describing, and categorizing — the critical language skills that power conversation.

Key Insight: Rich vocabulary includes not just names but features, functions, and categories. FFC cards build descriptive language that enables children to communicate meaningfully about their world.
Feature-Function-Class Cards — DIY Guide
Card Structure
  • Front: Clear picture of object
  • Back — FEATURE: What it looks like
  • Back — FUNCTION: What it does or is used for
  • Back — CLASS: What category it belongs to
Example — DOG
  • Feature: Has four legs, has fur, has a tail, can bark
  • Function: Guards the house, plays with family, goes for walks
  • Class: Animal, pet, mammal
Example — APPLE
  • Feature: Is red (or green), is round, has a stem, has seeds inside
  • Function: We eat it, it keeps us healthy
  • Class: Fruit, food, thing that grows on trees
Teaching Progression
  1. "What is it?" → "Apple." (basic label — mastered)
  1. "What group is apple in?" → "Fruit." (class)
  1. "What color is apple?" → "Red." (feature)
  1. "What do we do with apple?" → "Eat it." (function)
  1. "Tell me about apple." → "Apple is a fruit. It's red and round. We eat it."
Question Variety
  • Feature: What color? What shape? What size? Does it have fur?
  • Function: What do we do with it? Where do we use it?
  • Class: What group? What kind of thing? Is it a food or a toy?
Advanced FFC
  • Receptive FFC: "Give me the fruit that is yellow and we peel it."
  • Intraverbal FFC: "Name a fruit that is red."
Price Range: ₹400–1,200 commercial | Free (homemade)

Safety Note: Don't introduce FFC questions before basic labeling is solid. Build FFC on a foundation of successful simple labeling first.
Material 7 of 9
Natural Environment Teaching Kits
Labeling where real life happens.
Labeling taught only at the table with flashcards often fails to generalize to real life — the child labels a picture of a spoon but doesn't say "spoon" when using one at dinner. Natural Environment Teaching (NET) kits bring labeling practice into everyday contexts.
A kitchen NET kit includes real kitchen items for labeling during cooking. A bath NET kit includes bath toys and items for labeling during bath time. A park NET kit includes outdoor items for labeling during outside play. When labeling happens in the natural context where items are actually used, the learning is more meaningful and far more likely to transfer. Your child learns to label "spoon" not just when shown a flashcard, but when using a spoon to eat, when putting spoons away, when setting the table.

Key Insight: Generalization is the ultimate goal. Labels learned in natural contexts transfer better than table-taught labels alone. Real life is the best classroom.
Natural Environment Teaching Kits — DIY Guide
Kit Contents by Location
  • Kitchen Kit: Spoon, fork, plate, cup, bowl, pot, pan, lid — foods during cooking/eating — actions: cut, pour, stir, mix
  • Bath Kit: Soap, shampoo, towel, water, toys — body parts during washing — actions: wash, pour, splash, dry
  • Bedroom Kit: Pillow, blanket, bed, light, clothes — actions: sleep, wake up, dress
  • Outdoor/Park Kit: Ball, swing, slide, sand, tree, flower, bird — weather items — actions: run, jump, climb, throw
  • Mealtime Kit: Every food served, utensils used — actions: eat, drink, chew, bite
Teaching Strategies in Natural Context
  • Incidental teaching: Wait for child's interest, then prompt label
  • Embedded teaching: During routine, pause for labeling opportunity
  • Natural reinforcement: Labeling the juice leads to getting the juice
  • Modeling: Constant labeling by parents during all activities
Example Kitchen Session
While cooking: "What's this? Pot! The pot is hot. What are we doing? Stirring. What's in the pot? Soup!"
While eating: "What do you want? Apple? Say apple! Great labeling!"
While cleaning: "Let's put away the… spoon. What else? Fork!"
Tracking Natural Context Labels
  • Note which contexts labels are used in
  • Target labels known receptively but not used expressively in natural context
  • Celebrate every spontaneous label in a natural context!
Price Range: ₹300–1,500 depending on items | Most items already in the home

Safety Note: Never compromise safety for teaching opportunities. Don't practice labeling near hot stoves, in traffic, or in any unsafe conditions.
Material 8 of 9
Action and Verb Picture Cards
Verbs are words too.
Most early labeling programs focus almost exclusively on nouns — naming objects. But verbs (action words) are equally important for language development and are significantly undertaught. Action picture cards show people or animals doing things: running, jumping, eating, sleeping, reading, playing.
Learning to label actions expands language from "ball" to "throw ball," from "boy" to "boy running." Action labels support following and giving directions, narrating activities, and describing events. Many children with language delays have significantly weaker verb vocabularies than noun vocabularies. Explicit action labeling practice closes this gap and enables the more complete sentences that make real communication possible.

Key Insight: Verbs are the engines of sentences. Building action vocabulary enables children to describe what's happening — not just what exists. Nouns + verbs = sentences.
Action and Verb Picture Cards — DIY Guide
Action Categories to Include
  • Gross motor: running, jumping, walking, climbing, swimming, dancing
  • Fine motor: cutting, drawing, writing, building
  • Daily living: eating, drinking, sleeping, brushing, washing
  • Play: playing, throwing, catching, kicking, swinging
  • Social: hugging, waving, talking, sharing
  • Household: cooking, cleaning, opening, closing
Card Design
  • Clear action in progress — not ambiguous
  • Person or animal clearly performing the action
  • Simple background, one action per card
  • Use real photos of family members doing actions when possible
Teaching Verbs
  • "What is she doing?" → "Running!"
  • "What am I doing?" (adult models action) → "Jumping!"
  • "What are you doing?" (child does action) → "Eating!"
Combining Nouns and Verbs
Once nouns and verbs are established separately:
  • "What is the boy doing?" → "Boy running."
  • "Who is eating?" → "Girl eating."
  • "What is the dog doing?" → "Dog sleeping."
  • Expand: "The boy is running fast."
Progression
  1. Label action in picture
  1. Label action modeled by adult
  1. Label own action while doing it
  1. Combine noun + verb: "Boy running"
  1. Expand to full sentence: "The boy is running fast"
Price Range: ₹300–700

Safety Note: Balance seated picture labeling with active, movement-based verb practice for optimal engagement.
Material 9 of 9
Labeling Prompt Hierarchy Visual
How to help — and when to step back.
Effective labeling teaching requires knowing when and how to prompt. A prompt hierarchy visual shows parents and therapists the sequence of prompts from most support to least, preventing two common traps: getting stuck at heavy prompting forever, or removing support too quickly and causing a cascade of errors.
Having this visual posted in the therapy area, on the fridge, or wherever labeling practice happens is a powerful reminder for every caregiver. It makes the science of systematic prompting visible and actionable — so every adult working with the child is using the same approach, creating consistency that accelerates learning.

Key Insight: Systematic prompting and fading is the science of teaching. The hierarchy ensures your child receives support without developing dependence on that support.
Labeling Prompt Hierarchy Visual — DIY Guide
Prompt Levels — Most to Least Support
  1. FULL MODEL: Say the complete word. "Ball. Say ball."
  1. PARTIAL MODEL: Say part of the word. "Ba…" and wait.
  1. INITIAL SOUND: Give just the first sound. "/b/…"
  1. VISUAL CUE: Point to mouth, show picture of first sound position
  1. EXPECTANT PAUSE: Wait with expectant expression — no verbal support
  1. INDEPENDENT: Child responds without any prompt
Visual Format
  • Vertical chart showing all levels clearly
  • Color coding: green = most support, fading toward independent
  • Icons for each level for visual clarity
  • Posted wherever teaching happens
Using the Hierarchy
  • Start with enough support for success (80%+ correct per session)
  • Track which prompt level child needs for each word
  • Fade prompts systematically — don't skip levels
  • If errors increase, go back up one level
Prompt Fading Procedure
  1. Child responds correctly 3+ times at one level
  1. Try the next level (less support)
  1. If child errors, provide correct model and return to previous level
  1. Goal: move every word toward independent
Additional Prompt Types
  • Time delay: Pause before prompting to give opportunity to try independently
  • Transfer trial: After prompted response, immediately re-present for independent trial
  • Echoic prompt: "Ball. What is it?"
Price Range: Free (homemade poster)

Safety Note: Prompts support success — they must not create permanent dependence. Always have a fading plan. Prompt hierarchies are tools for progress, not permanent scaffolds.

Clinical Reference — Key Principles Summary
For therapists and program leads: a synthesis of the core clinical principles underlying effective labeling instruction.
1
Tact Definition
A tact is a verbal operant where a response is evoked by a nonverbal stimulus (seeing a ball → "Ball!") and reinforced by generalized social consequences. Unlike manding, tacting is stimulus-controlled, not motivation-controlled.
2
Errorless Learning Rationale
Errors can become learned responses. Preventing errors from the start — through immediate prompting and high success rates (80%+) — maintains motivation and builds correct response patterns efficiently.
3
Prompt Dependence Prevention
Every word must have a prompt fading plan. Use transfer trials, time delay, and differential reinforcement to systematically move each label toward independence. Track prompt level per word with data.
4
Generalization is the Goal
Program for generalization from the start: multiple exemplars, multiple settings, multiple listeners, multiple conditions. A label known only in the therapy room has limited functional value.
5
Assessment-Driven Programming
VB-MAPP, ABLLS-R, or similar tools identify tact repertoire and guide target selection. Vocabulary selection should be functional, motivating, frequent, motor-feasible, and varied across categories.
Vocabulary Selection — What to Teach First
Not all words are equal when building a labeling program. Evidence-based vocabulary selection criteria ensure teaching time is spent on the words that will make the biggest difference in the child's life.
Category Balance in Early Programs
  • Nouns: Most early labeling — objects, people, animals
  • Verbs: Often undertaught but critical for sentences
  • Adjectives: Color, size, shape, texture — descriptive labeling
  • Prepositions: In, on, under — spatial relationships
  • Social words: Names of people, greetings
Milestones to Track
  • Early: 10 labels with full prompts → 10 independently → 25 → 50 → 100
  • Intermediate: Labels nouns AND verbs → uses FFC → category labels → 200+
  • Advanced: Spontaneous commenting → novel labeling → descriptive language
Generalization — Making Labels Stick in Real Life
A label learned in one context with one person on one type of flashcard is not a fully functional label. Systematic generalization programming ensures the child's labels work everywhere they need to.
Across Exemplars
Teach "dog" using many different dogs — big, small, furry, short-haired, different colors. Child should label all dogs, not just one familiar picture.
Across Settings
Practice labels in the therapy room, at home, at the park, in the grocery store. Real generalization means labeling anywhere the object appears.
Across Listeners
Practice with therapist, parent, grandparent, sibling, and strangers. Labels should work for any communication partner — not just the teaching therapist.
Spontaneous Labeling
The ultimate goal: child labels unprompted when encountering objects in the environment. "Look, Mama — bird!" without anyone asking.

Receptive vs. Expressive Labeling
Understanding the distinction between comprehension and verbal production is fundamental for effective language intervention. Children often demonstrate a significant gap, understanding many more words than they can spontaneously say.
Receptive Labeling (Understanding)
This refers to a child's ability to comprehend spoken language. It's an internal process, demonstrated by actions like pointing to requested objects, following instructions, or identifying pictures. Receptive skills typically develop earlier and are often more robust than expressive abilities, forming the crucial foundation for later language production.
Expressive Labeling (Producing/Tacting)
Expressive labeling, or tacting, is the act of spontaneously naming or describing items, actions, or events. This skill demands more complex vocal and motor planning. The aim is for the child to use words independently to comment on their environment, transitioning from simply knowing what something is to actively communicating about it.
Bridging this gap requires systematic teaching strategies, including prompt fading and extensive generalization, to help children move from comprehension to independent verbal expression.
