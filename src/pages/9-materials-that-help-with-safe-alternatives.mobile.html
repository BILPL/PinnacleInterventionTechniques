What Happens in the Brain
Sensory overload signal — amygdala fires a "danger" alert to the entire nervous system
Prefrontal cortex goes offline — rational thinking temporarily unavailable
Survival mode activates — fight, flight, or freeze response takes over
Behaviour as output — biting, hitting, crashing provides intense proprioceptive input
Regulatory relief achieved — nervous system calms; the behaviour worked
In Plain English
When your child bites, crashes, or scratches, their amygdala has fired a "danger" signal. The rational brain goes temporarily offline. The body enters survival mode. Intense proprioceptive input — biting, impact, deep pressure — does calm the nervous system. This is neurologically real.
The behaviour works. It provides the intense sensory input that cuts through the overload and brings the child back to baseline. They are not being bad. They are self-medicating a dysregulated nervous system with the only tools they currently have.

The clinical term: Functionally Equivalent Replacement Behaviours (FERBs). ABA principle: every behaviour serves a function. Replace the method, not the need.

Safe Alternative Provision
Parent-friendly: "Replace, Don't Remove"

Safe Alternative Provision is a clinically validated intervention approach that treats dangerous, self-injurious, or harmful behaviours as functional communications rather than mere misbehaviour. Instead of simply suppressing a behaviour — which leaves the underlying need unmet — this approach identifies what function the behaviour serves and provides a safer, functionally equivalent means to meet that same need.
A child who bites their arm needs intense oral proprioceptive input. Providing a chewable tool meets that need safely. A child who crashes into walls needs deep proprioceptive impact. A crash pad provides that impact without injury. The behaviour is replaced, not merely forbidden.

What Safe Alternative Provision Targets: Primary, Secondary, Long-Term
Safe Alternative Provision doesn't just stop a dangerous behaviour. It works on three simultaneous levels — each one building on the last. Understanding all three helps you measure real progress, not just surface calm.
1
🎯 Primary: Eliminate the Dangerous Behaviour
Replace self-biting, wall-crashing, head-banging, and scratching with a functionally equivalent safe alternative. The dangerous behaviour must be replaced — not suppressed. Suppression without replacement causes behaviour migration (the need finds a new, often worse, outlet).
Success metric: 50%+ reduction in dangerous behaviour frequency within 6–8 weeks.
2
🔄 Secondary: Build a Regulatory Toolkit
The child learns that their nervous system can be regulated through safe, socially acceptable means. Over time, they develop a personal toolkit — 3 to 5 go-to alternatives they reach for independently.
Success metric: Child initiates alternative use without adult prompt within 4–6 weeks of consistent exposure.
3
🌱 Long-Term: Nervous System Flexibility
The ultimate goal is not just replacement — it's nervous system flexibility. The child learns to tolerate increasing levels of sensory input before reaching dysregulation threshold. Dangerous behaviours become rare, then exceptional, then historical.
Success metric: Dangerous behaviour drops below 1 incident per week by Week 12. Child can self-regulate in 2+ environments (home + school).

⚠️ Clinical Note: If only the primary target is addressed without building the secondary toolkit, relapse rates are high. All three levels must be worked simultaneously. — Pinnacle Blooms Consortium, BCBA + OT + SLP Integrated Team

Material 7 in Depth: Safe Destruction Materials
Parent-friendly: "Give the urge somewhere safe to go."
Some children need to destroy, tear, crush, or break things. This is not aggression — it is a high-arousal regulatory need. The urge to rip, pop, or smash provides intense proprioceptive and tactile feedback that rapidly discharges nervous system overload. Safe Destruction Materials give this urge a legal, consequence-free outlet.
Make This (Free)
  • Cardboard destruction box: collect cereal boxes, packaging, egg cartons — child tears, crushes, and stomps freely
  • Newspaper shred pile: stack old newspapers, child rips into strips — deeply satisfying oral-motor + proprioceptive combo
  • Bubble wrap mat: tape bubble wrap to the floor, child jumps and stomps
  • Ice cube smash: place ice cubes in a zip-lock bag, child smashes with a wooden spoon on a cutting board
  • Playdough from flour + salt + water: pound and tear freely
The Destruction Box is the single most effective low-cost Safe Destruction tool. Designate one cardboard box as the "smash box." Fill it with egg cartons, cereal boxes, and scrap cardboard. When the child is escalating, point to the box: "Smash box is ready." No further instruction needed. Replace contents weekly. Cost: ₹0.

Clinical Boundary: Safe Destruction Materials are for designated materials only. The rule must be explicit and consistent: "We smash the smash box. We do not smash walls, furniture, or people." Pair every destruction session with a clear verbal boundary — stated calmly, not punitively. The boundary is part of the therapy. — Pinnacle Blooms Consortium, OT + BCBA Protocol
When to Use Safe Destruction (Timing Guide)

D-415 | Safety Protocol — Read Before Session 1
🛑 SAFETY FIRST. Read Before You Introduce Any Alternative.
Safe Alternative Provision is clinically validated and parent-friendly — but every material must pass a safety check before it reaches your child. This is not optional. One unsafe material can cause injury and destroy trust in the entire programme.
1
⚠️ Choking & Ingestion Risk
All chewable tools must be size-appropriate for the child's age and bite strength. Never use chewables smaller than 4 cm in any dimension for children under 8. Inspect every chew tool before each session — discard if cracked, torn, or showing bite-through. Never leave a child unsupervised with a chewable tool. Replace chew tubes every 4–6 weeks regardless of visible wear.
2
⚠️ Impact & Compression Risk
Crash pads must be a minimum of 10 cm deep foam — never use thin gym mats as a substitute. Weighted blankets must not exceed 10% of the child's body weight. Never apply weighted items to the neck, face, or chest. Compression vests must allow full chest expansion — two fingers must fit under the vest at all times. Remove all weighted items after 20 minutes maximum.
3
⚠️ Sensory Overload Risk
Introduce only ONE new alternative per session. Never introduce two new materials simultaneously — the nervous system cannot process competing novel inputs. If the child shows distress signals (turning away, vocalising, pushing material away), stop immediately. Distress is not resistance — it is communication. Honour it. Wait 48 hours before re-offering a rejected material.

🛡️ MEDICAL CLEARANCE FIRST: Before beginning Safe Alternative Provision, rule out organic causes of self-injurious behaviour — dental pain, GI discomfort, ear infections, and skin conditions can all drive self-injury. A paediatrician sign-off is required before Session 1. If your child has a known medical condition affecting sensation, consult your OT before introducing any new material. — Pinnacle Blooms Consortium, Safety Protocol SP-001

Resistance Protocol
If child ignores or rejects: place item nearby and leave it. Come back in 5 minutes. Do not repeat the offer more than twice in one window. The goal today is exposure, not use.
Step 02 of 6
Step 2: The Engagement. Match the Intensity the Child Is Seeking.
Timing: 1–3 minutes
The most common mistake at this stage is offering a material that is too gentle. If your child bites hard enough to bruise, a soft silicone chew ring will not satisfy the need. You must match — or slightly exceed — the intensity of the behaviour you are replacing.
Chewable Engagement
If child is an oral seeker: offer the hardest-grade chew tool available. Let them bite freely. Do not redirect or comment on the biting. Say: "That one's really tough — good for hard chewing." Silence is fine. Presence is enough.
Crash Space Engagement
If child is an impact seeker: physically demonstrate crashing into the pad yourself first. Make it look satisfying. Say: "Watch — like this." Then step back. Do not instruct. Let the child's body lead.
Squeeze & Pressure Engagement
If child is a proprioceptive seeker: place therapy putty or a stress ball in their hands and apply gentle co-active pressure together. Say: "Squeeze as hard as you can." Count to five aloud. Release. Repeat.
Communication Tool Engagement
If child is a communicative behaviour seeker: role-play a scenario where you use the card yourself. Say: "This is getting too loud for me — I'm going to use my card." Hold it up. Pause. "See? Now I feel better." Hand the child their card.
Intensity Matching Guide
  • Soft biting → Medium-grade chew tube
  • Hard biting / bruising → Hard or extra-hard grade chewelry
  • Occasional wall touch → Firm crash pad, 1–2 jumps
  • Running at walls / floors → Deep crash pad, full-body impact
  • Light hand-wringing → Stress ball, light resistance putty
  • Hitting self / others → Therapy putty, hard grade, heavy work
What NOT to Do at This Stage
  • Do not praise or comment on the alternative use yet — reinforcement comes in Step 5
  • Do not redirect to a different activity ("let's do puzzles instead")
  • Do not reduce the intensity to make yourself more comfortable
  • Do not end the engagement before the child is ready — watch for natural satiation signals (slowing down, releasing the material, looking away)

When the Alternative Isn't Working. It's Not Failure — It's Diagnostic Information.
"My child refuses every alternative offered."
The function hasn't been correctly identified yet. A chewable won't replace head banging if head banging provides whole-body proprioceptive input, not oral input. Re-assess which sensory modality the dangerous behaviour targets. Consult OT for formal sensory assessment. 📞 9100 181 181
"My child uses the alternative but still does the dangerous behaviour too."
This is NORMAL in weeks 1–4. The alternative is being learned. The dangerous behaviour has years of neural pathway strength. Track: is the dangerous behaviour decreasing over weeks? Even 20% reduction is success. Continue consistently.
"My child uses the alternative in calm states but not during crisis."
The alternative hasn't been practised enough during mild escalation to be accessible in full crisis. Practice during low-intensity states first. The neural pathway to the alternative must be well-worn before crisis hits.
"The chewable isn't satisfying — child keeps biting themselves."
The chewable resistance is wrong. If child bites with significant force, they need FIRM resistance — ARK Therapeutic hard grade or frozen items. Soft chewables are insufficient for high-intensity oral seekers.
"The crash pad isn't interesting to my child."
Try adding a trampoline. Try whole-body rolling rather than jumping. Try adding jumping targets — small beanbags to land on. The proprioceptive need may require more sustained input than a single crash provides.
"The communication card isn't being used during actual overwhelm."
The break must be HONOURED EVERY TIME in early learning. If even once the child used the card and didn't get a break, the tool lost credibility. Reset: honour 100% for the next 2 weeks. Then slowly teach that sometimes breaks must wait 30 seconds.

One Size Does Not Serve Every Nervous System. Here's How to Adapt.
Sensory Seeker (High Input Needed)
  • Use HARD GRADE chewables — soft ones won't satisfy
  • Crash pad should allow FULL body impact — don't restrict
  • Squeeze tools: therapy putty at highest resistance
  • Heavy work: weighted carrying, maximum proprioceptive input
  • Destruction: large cardboard boxes, not just paper
Sensory Avoider (Cautious About New Sensations)
  • Introduce alternatives extremely gradually — no rushing
  • Start with LIGHT textures: smooth chewable, gentle squeeze
  • Never force — acceptance may take 4–8 weeks of exposure
  • Compression: start with firm blanket wrap before weighted items
  • Communication tools may be the primary alternative first
Ages 2–4
Keep alternatives ON THE BODY. Adult-initiated, passive availability — no teaching needed. Crash corner always set up.
Ages 5–8
Begin teaching self-recognition. Practice naming the need. Introduce choice: "Chew necklace or pencil topper?"
Ages 9–12
Collaborative selection. Portable, discrete options for school. Self-monitoring tools and personal check-in cards.
Ages 12–14
Normalised alternatives: gym, gum, cold water. Respect autonomy. Self-directed regulation with adult available as backup.

D-415 Is One Technique in a Complete Journey. Here's Where You Are and Where You're Heading.
Safe Alternative Provision (D-415) addresses the immediate danger. But your child's nervous system needs more than one technique. Below is the clinical progression pathway — where D-415 sits, what it unlocks, and what comes next once the dangerous behaviour is under control.
1
📍 You Are Here: D-415 — Safe Alternative Provision
Weeks 1–8. Replace dangerous self-injurious behaviour with functionally equivalent safe alternatives. Goal: 50%+ reduction in dangerous behaviour. Unlocks: nervous system is now accessible for deeper therapeutic work.
2
➡️ Next: D-416 — Sensory Diet Design
Weeks 6–12 (overlaps with D-415). Build a full-day proactive sensory schedule so the nervous system never reaches crisis threshold. 8–10 sensory inputs distributed across the day. Goal: reduce reactive episodes by 70%. Requires: D-415 alternatives already accepted.
3
➡️ Then: D-420 — Emotional Regulation Foundations
Weeks 10–20. Introduce body-based emotional awareness — the child learns to identify their own arousal level before it becomes dangerous. Uses zones of regulation, interoception activities, and co-regulation scripts. Requires: child tolerating adult proximity during dysregulation (built in D-415).
4
➡️ Then: D-431 — Self-Advocacy & Communication
Weeks 16–30. Child learns to communicate regulatory needs using words, symbols, or AAC before the behaviour occurs. "I need a break." "Too loud." "Squeeze please." Requires: stable alternative behaviour established (D-415 + D-416 complete).
5
🏁 Destination: Independent Self-Regulation
Month 6–12+. Child independently identifies dysregulation, selects an appropriate tool, uses it, and returns to baseline — without adult prompting. Dangerous behaviour is historical. This is the clinical goal of the entire programme.
