The Neuroscience of Public-Private Understanding — Plain English
The Abstract Concept Problem
"Public" and "private" are abstract social constructs. Neurotypical children absorb these rules through social osmosis. The autistic brain processes social information differently — it requires explicit teaching of rules that others learn implicitly.
The Inhibitory Control Gap
Executive function — the brain's "pause and consider" system — matures more slowly in many children with autism. Your child is not choosing to misbehave. Their brain is doing exactly what its current wiring allows.
The Sensory Dimension
For many children, particularly younger ones, genital touching serves a sensory-seeking function — not a sexual one. The genitourinary area is richly innervated. Some children touch this area the same way others chew their sleeves: for sensory input. Occupational therapy assessment distinguishes sensory-seeking from sexual behaviour.
The Teaching Imperative
What neurotypical children absorb socially must be explicitly, visually, and repeatedly taught to children with autism. This is not a failure — it is a pedagogical reality. And it is entirely solvable.

Public-Private Boundary Teaching — What It Is

Parent Alias: "Teaching Where Private Things Belong"
Public-Private Boundary Teaching is a multi-modal, dignity-preserving intervention approach that systematically teaches children with autism and developmental disabilities the social rule that certain behaviours — including touching one's genitals — belong exclusively in private settings. The approach does not attempt to eliminate a developmentally normal behaviour. It teaches appropriate contextual discrimination: the understanding that this behaviour is acceptable AND that it belongs in specific, private locations.
Intervention integrates visual teaching systems, social narratives, consistent behavioural protocols, sensory assessment, and proactive body education — delivered by parents at home, coordinated with the therapy team, and monitored through GPT-OS®.

The 9 Materials That Help — What to Get, Where to Get It
These 9 materials address D-422 through five clinical pathways: visual teaching, narrative learning, sensory regulation, environmental design, and behavioural reinforcement. Not all are needed simultaneously — your therapy team will guide sequencing.
1. Public-Private Visual Sorting System
Laminated card sets depicting PLACES and BEHAVIORS. Child physically sorts which behaviours go with which places using a green (private) / red (public) sorting mat. Makes abstract social concepts concrete and physically manipulable.
2. Social Stories™ for Private Behaviours
A short, first-person narrative explaining the social rule in concrete, non-judgmental language, customised to the child's specific settings and language level. NCAEP (2020) Evidence-Based Practice for autism.
3. Private Place Visual Cue Cards
A small laminated card showing a bedroom door or bathroom symbol. When the child begins the behaviour in public, the caregiver calmly shows the card — no verbal correction needed. Visual cues are classified as Evidence-Based Practice (NCAEP 2020).
4. Body Parts & Boundaries Education Curriculum
Adapted sexuality education materials that teach correct anatomical names, the concept of "private parts," and who can touch which body parts. Provides the foundational conceptual framework that makes public-private rules comprehensible.
5. Sensory Replacement Toolkit
A collection of sensory tools providing deep pressure, tactile, or proprioceptive input — meeting similar sensory needs through appropriate means. Includes weighted blanket, compression vest, fidget tools, textured objects, putty/clay, and movement activities.
6. Environmental Modifications for Privacy Access
Ensuring the child has actual access to a private space and knows exactly how to get there. The "private space pathway" is physically taught: feel urge → show card/say word → walk to bedroom → close door. Access to privacy is prerequisite to compliance with privacy rules.
7. Consistent Redirection Protocol (Written)
A one-page written protocol that ALL caregivers follow. Notice behaviour → Show visual cue card → Brief verbal: "Private place" → Guide child → Allow in private space → Reinforce. Consistency across all caregivers is the single most critical factor.
8. Reinforcement System
A positive reinforcement system that catches and rewards CORRECT behaviour: going to private space independently, responding to the cue card, requesting "private time." Behaviour grows through reinforcement — build what you want by catching and rewarding it.
Material 9: Proactive Sexuality Education & Counselling
For older children and adolescents — comprehensive, adapted sexuality education delivered by a trained professional. Addresses developing sexuality, appropriate expression, body autonomy and consent, relationship safety, staying safe from inappropriate touch by others, puberty changes. This is distinct from behaviour management — it is rights-based education.

Why It Matters: Individuals with developmental disabilities are significantly more vulnerable to sexual abuse partly because of inadequate sexuality education. Proactive education prevents problems, protects the child, and is an established right per AAIDD and WHO.
When to Use: Do not wait for problems. Begin age-appropriate body safety education early. Expand into comprehensive sexuality education as child matures.

Before You Begin — Read This Carefully
🔴 RED: Do Not Proceed — Seek Professional Assessment First
  • Child is showing the behaviour WITH other people (touching others) → Immediate behaviour analyst consultation
  • Any sign of exposure to inappropriate sexual content or contact → Mandatory assessment + POCSO Act reporting consideration
  • Sudden onset of new behaviour with no developmental history → Medical evaluation first (rule out UTI, skin condition, pain)
  • Child shows distress during the behaviour (crying, self-injury) → Medical + psychiatric assessment
  • Child's communication level does not allow understanding of "private" in any modality → Specialised assessment first
🟡 AMBER: Modify Approach
  • Child is under 4 years → Use very simple body safety language only; consult OT for sensory assessment
  • Child is severely intellectually disabled → Adapt to simplest possible visual system; increase professional supervision
  • Caregivers are in significant disagreement → Resolve caregiver alignment FIRST; inconsistency undermines all progress
  • Child has recent trauma history → Coordinate with mental health professional before implementing structured protocol
🟢 GREEN: Proceed with Standard D-422 Protocol
  • Child is engaging in self-touching in inappropriate locations (not directed at others)
  • Medical causes have been ruled out (or are not indicated)
  • Child has cognitive level sufficient to learn a concrete rule with visual supports
  • All primary caregivers are willing to implement consistent protocol
Stop everything and call 9100 181 181 if: the behaviour is directed at another child or adult, you suspect your child has been sexually abused, or you observe signs of physical injury or infection.

Step 1 of 6
Step 1: The Invitation
Duration: 30–60 seconds | Purpose: The child is brought into the teaching activity through playful, low-demand engagement — not a command.
"Hey [Child's name] — want to play our sorting game? Let's see where things go!"
For lower-language children: Hold up sorting mat with a smile. Point to it. Wait 5 seconds for approach.
What Acceptance Looks Like
  • Child approaches or looks at materials
  • Child extends hand toward sorting cards
  • Child makes eye contact with materials
What Resistance Looks Like — and How to Respond
  • Child moves away → "That's okay. Want to sit with me for a minute?"
  • Child ignores → Wait, then try sensory invitation: offer preferred fidget first, then introduce sorting
  • Child protests → "Okay, not now. We'll try again after [preferred activity]."
Pairing Principle
If a child is reluctant, pair the sorting activity with a highly preferred item for 3–5 sessions before introducing teaching content. The activity must be associated with positive experience.
Step 2 of 6
Step 2: The Engagement
Duration: 1–3 minutes | Purpose: Child is now interacting with the material. The therapeutic teaching begins.
"Look — this is our [green mat]. These are PRIVATE places — places where things are just for us. And this is our [red mat]. These are PUBLIC places — places with other people. Let's sort these together."
Material Introduction: Start with PLACE cards only (first 3–5 sessions). Hold up bedroom card → "Bedroom — private or public?" → Wait 5 seconds → Guide to green if needed → "Yes! Bedroom is private!" Then introduce school card → Guide to red → "Right! School is public, with other people!"
Reinforcement Cue — immediately after each correct sort: "Yes! [Child's name] sorted it! Bedroom is PRIVATE! Great sorting!" — then deliver preferred reinforcer from menu.
Engagement
Child reaches for cards, looks at mats, vocalises — continue
Tolerance
Child participates minimally — continue, reduce session length
Avoidance
Child repeatedly pulls away — end session positively, try again tomorrow

Key Principle: The first goal is TOLERATING the activity. Learning comes later. Never push through avoidance.
Step 3 of 6 — The Core
Step 3: The Therapeutic Action
Duration: 5–10 minutes | Purpose: This is the active ingredient — explicit teaching of the public-private discrimination rule.
Phase A: Place Sorting (Sessions 1–10)
Sort ALL place cards into green (private) / red (public). Bedroom, bathroom, locked toilet = private. School, store, restaurant, park, car, living room = public.
Phase B: Behaviour Sorting (Sessions 11–20)
"Touching private parts" card → green mat. Same neutral, matter-of-fact tone as "getting dressed." Same category. Same tone. No special emphasis that signals shame.
Phase C: In-The-Moment Referencing (Sessions 15+)
Show cue card calmly → brief: "[Name] — private place." → Guide to bedroom → Allow in private space → No further comment → Return to activity → Reinforce if child goes independently.
Common Execution Errors
  • ❌ Delivering long explanation during the behaviour (not processed)
  • ❌ Whispering with embarrassment (children read your emotional state)
  • ❌ Looking around to see if others noticed (communicates shame)
  • ❌ Using shame language: "That's bad / dirty / wrong" (psychologically harmful)
Practise saying this 10 times before you need it:
"Private place."
In a neutral, teaching voice. Not embarrassed. Not angry. Like you'd say "wash hands" or "shoes on."
Citation: PMC10955541 | NCAEP (2020) | ABA Antecedent-Based Intervention
Step 4 of 6
Step 4: Repeat & Vary
Duration: 3–5 minutes | Therapeutic Dosage: Sorting sessions 5–10 cards per session, 2–3 sessions per week. Social story: read DAILY. In-the-moment cue: every single time without exception.
Add new environments
After mastering basic sorting, add NEW public and private places — cinema, temple, beach, relative's home — one at a time.
Role-play scenarios
"Let's pretend we're at the park. What would we do if we felt like touching our private parts?" → Child shows/says: "Private place!"
Reverse sorting
Parent makes a mistake → child corrects parent. This empowering exercise demonstrates deep learning and consolidates the rule.
Read satiation signals
Looking away repeatedly, pushing cards away, vocal protesting, motor restlessness — end the session positively before resistance peaks.
"3 Good Reps > 10 Forced Reps — end on a successful sort. Never push through satiation. Quality of engagement matters more than quantity of repetitions."

Step 5: Reinforce & Celebrate
Throughout session | Reinforcement timing: within 3 seconds of desired behaviour.
1
Priority 1 — Highest Value
Child independently goes to private space when urge arises. Reinforce immediately, enthusiastically, every single time.
2
Priority 2
Child responds to visual cue card and relocates to private space. "You saw the card and went to your room — that's exactly right!"
3
Priority 3
Child correctly sorts a behaviour card to private place. Praise + token/sticker.

Child requests "private time" verbally, via AAC, or via sign. This is self-regulation — give it your highest reinforcement value!

What NOT to Reinforce: Do not provide extra attention, comfort, or discussion DURING or AFTER the behaviour in a public setting. Attention is a reinforcer. Calm, brief, neutral redirection — then move on.

Step 6 of 6
Step 6: The Cool-Down
Duration: 1–2 minutes | Purpose: Every formal teaching session ends with a predictable, calming transition. The child is not left in therapeutic engagement mode.
"Two more cards, then we're all done! One more... and... ALL DONE! 🌟 Great work!"
Cool-Down Activity (choose one)
  • Child puts sorting cards away in the box (participatory closure)
  • 60-second preferred activity (small toy, iPad clip)
  • Parent reads one page of social story as calming wind-down
  • Physical: shake hands, high-five, squeeze hands
If Child Resists Ending
"Box is sleeping. It will wake up tomorrow. Now it's [preferred activity] time." Show visual schedule.
After the Session
Return to normal family routine. No debrief. No lengthy praise. Session is complete. The learning is in the nervous system now — let it consolidate.

Reality Check — Most Sessions Aren't Perfect. Here's Your Fix.
❓ Child refuses to engage with sorting cards at all
Why: Materials not yet paired with positive experience. Fix: Pair sorting cards with highest preferred item for 5 sessions (no teaching demand). Just: "Play with cards → get [favourite thing]." Then reintroduce sorting slowly.
❓ Child keeps "incorrectly" sorting touching private parts to the PUBLIC mat
Why: Child is reporting reality — this is not noncompliance, it's honest sorting. Fix: "That's where you do it now. AND — here's where it GOES." Guide to private mat with enthusiastic reinforcement. The rule teaches the desired location, not the current one.
❓ Child had a public incident despite knowing the sorting game well
Why: Knowing a rule ≠ applying it under arousal/sensory drive. The in-the-moment inhibitory pathway is slower to develop than sorting game performance. Fix: Normal. Do NOT punish. Calmly redirect. Note in data. Increase in-the-moment practice in low-stakes environments.
❓ Grandparent/teacher responded with shame before you could stop them
Why: Cultural discomfort; insufficient caregiver training. Fix: Do NOT correct in front of child. Address privately. Provide written protocol. Consider school meeting. If consistent implementation isn't achievable across this caregiver, limit their role in this intervention.
❓ Child has been doing well for 2 weeks then regressed
Why: Regression is normal in learning curves. May be stress-related. Skill is not yet consolidated. Fix: Return to Phase A sorting. Increase social story frequency. Check for stressors. Normal developmental noise — not programme failure.
❓ Behaviour is happening MORE frequently since we started
Why: Possible extinction burst — behaviour temporarily increases when a previously reinforced response stops being reinforced. Normal in early ABA implementation. Fix: Stay consistent. Extinction bursts resolve with consistent protocol. Contact your BCBA if it persists beyond 2 weeks.

Emergency Response: If child becomes severely distressed during the redirect — abandon redirect, attend to distress, do NOT discuss the behaviour, document what happened, contact Pinnacle team. Helpline: 9100 181 181

Even After Progress — Stay Alert to These Signals
🚨 Behaviour Directed at Another Person
Child attempts to touch another person's private areas or exposes self in front of others in an interactive way. Requires immediate comprehensive assessment — different intervention framework, potential safety concerns. Contact Pinnacle team immediately: 9100 181 181
🚨 Sudden Significant Regression After Stable Period
Behaviour frequency doubles within 1 week after 4+ weeks of progress. May signal new stressor, environmental change, or social safety concern. Teleconsult within 48 hours. Assess for environmental changes; rule out abuse.
🚨 Child Develops Shame, Anxiety, or Distress Related to Their Body
Child flinches at body-related language, stops toileting privately, expresses shame. Protocol may have inadvertently introduced shame. Review caregiver protocol immediately; consult clinical psychologist; reorient to non-shaming approach.
🚨 Signs of Possible Sexual Abuse or Inappropriate Exposure
New sexualised behaviour, knowledge beyond developmental level, fear responses around specific adults, physical signs. Safety emergency. Mandatory assessment. POCSO Act reporting obligation in India. Call 9100 181 181

"The Sensory Discovery"
Child: 7-year-old girl, autism + sensory processing disorder, Bengaluru
"She was touching herself constantly at the dinner table. We tried everything. Our OT assessed her and found the behaviour was primarily sensory-seeking, not sexual."
Protocol used: Sensory alternatives toolkit (compression vest, fidget tools, proprioceptive heavy work) PLUS visual sorting system. OT-led sensory diet significantly reduced frequency before the rule was even taught.
"The compression vest during meals reduced the behaviour by about 60%. Then the sorting system handled the rest. The sensory assessment was the key that unlocked everything."
Clinical Note: This case demonstrates why OT assessment for sensory function is mandatory before or alongside D-422.

Frequently Asked Questions — D-422
Is masturbation normal in children with autism?
Yes. Masturbation is a developmentally typical behaviour that occurs across all children, including those with autism. The challenge is not the behaviour itself — it is that children with autism often struggle with the abstract social concept of "private" behaviour. Our intervention teaches WHERE, not whether.
My child is very young (4–5 years). Is this appropriate to address?
At ages 4–5, the approach is very simple: body part names, basic body safety, and a simple spatial concept (bedroom = private). No complex sorting systems yet. Consult your OT and BCBA for age-appropriate protocol.
Should I punish my child for this behaviour in public?
No. Punishment creates shame, does not teach the correct behaviour, and may cause psychological harm to a child who genuinely does not understand the social rule. Calm, consistent, neutral redirection to a private space is the evidence-based response. Every time. Without exception.
What if my child doesn't have access to a private space at school?
This is an IEP/behaviour support plan issue. Your BCBA should work with the school to identify a designated private space (quiet room, bathroom) that the child can access when needed. This is a reasonable accommodation. We provide school communication templates — see the Share card.

More Frequently Asked Questions — D-422
What if the behaviour seems to be happening for sensory reasons, not sexual ones?
Request an OT sensory assessment immediately. For sensory-seeking behaviour, the intervention emphasis shifts significantly toward sensory alternatives (Material 5). Both approaches are needed, but the relative weight changes. This is why OT is a mandatory discipline in D-422.
My teenage child is now doing this. Is it too late?
It is never too late, but the approach changes with adolescence. For teenagers, comprehensive adapted sexuality education (Material 9) becomes the primary track. A BCBA + adapted sexuality educator combination is needed. The stakes are higher (legal, social), but the skills are entirely teachable. Contact 9100 181 181 for adolescent-specific protocol.
How do I talk to my child about this without creating shame?
Use the language of the social story: "Touching your private parts is okay. Private places are for private things." Matter-of-fact, calm, neutral. If you are embarrassed, your child will read it. Practise saying these words until they feel normal. Your calm teaches your child calm.
How long does D-422 typically take?
With consistent protocol implementation, most children show measurable progress within 4–8 weeks. Independent self-regulation typically emerges by 8–16 weeks. Complex cases may take longer. Individual results vary — track your child's specific progress against the milestones in the Progress Arc cards.
