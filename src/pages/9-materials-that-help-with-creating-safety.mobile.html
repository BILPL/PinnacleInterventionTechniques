Material 1: Visual Schedules & Daily Routines

🗓️ Step 1 of 9 — See What's Coming Next Visual schedules transform abstract time into concrete, visible sequence. Children who cannot hold schedules in memory — or whose anxiety prevents them from trusting remembered information — can look at the schedule and see what's coming. The schedule becomes an external brain for sequencing — removing the burden of mental tracking and reducing the anxiety of uncertainty. The Invitation Script "Let's look at our plan together." (Point to the schedule.) "See? First we do [Activity A]." (Point.) "Then [Activity B]. Then [Activity C]. You can always look here." Tone: Calm. Matter-of-fact. Not anxious. Not overexplaining. The schedule itself carries the safety — your voice anchors it. Acceptance Cues: Child approaches, looks, stops asking questions, checks independently. Resistance Cues: Child won't look → use photos of actual activities, not symbols. Start with just 3 items. Science & Products Working memory impairments common in autism mean verbal schedules fade within seconds. Visual schedules are persistent, reliable, and always accessible — answering 'What's next?' without requiring adult repetition. Material 2: Transition Warnings & Countdown Tools
⏱️ Step 2 of 9 — No More Surprise Endings
Transitions are the most vulnerable moments for predictability-dependent children. The end of one activity and the beginning of another creates a gap of uncertainty that triggers the threat response. Transition warnings close this gap by making upcoming change expected rather than sudden. The problem is never the transition itself. The problem is the surprise.
01
10-Minute Warning
"Ten minutes until we leave." Show timer starting.
02
5-Minute Warning
"Five minutes. See the timer?" Point to visual display.
03
2-Minute Warning
"Almost time. Two minutes." Keep tone calm and matter-of-fact.
04
1-Minute Warning
"One more minute." Child can now anticipate.
05
The Transition
"Time to go. You knew this was coming." Acknowledge the preparation explicitly.

⚠️ Critical Rule: If you say 5 minutes, honor 5 minutes. Inaccurate warnings increase anxiety — they teach the child that warnings cannot be trusted. The timer is the most important consistency tool you have.

Material 3: Social Stories for Upcoming Events
📖 Step 3 of 9 — Preview Before You Experience
New events trigger threat responses because they lack the predictability of routine. Social stories create predictability for non-routine events by describing in advance exactly what will happen, in what order, what the child can expect to see, hear, do, and feel. The brain fears the unknown. Social stories convert unknown events into known sequences before the child has to live them.
How to Write a Social Story
Structure: Setting → Who will be there → What will happen in order → What the child can do → How it will end → What comes after
Example: "Tomorrow we are going to Grandma's house. We will drive for about one hour. When we arrive, Grandma will open the door. She might want to hug me. I can hug her, or I can wave... After lunch, I can play in Grandma's garden. When it's time to go, I will hear 'five minutes.' Then we will drive home. After, I will be back in my own house with my own things."
The Accuracy Rule
Stories must reflect what will actually happen. Don't sanitize difficult parts. Accurate prediction builds trust. Inaccurate prediction damages trust in all future previews.

Material 4: Consistent Environmental Organization
🗂️ Step 4 of 9 — Everything in Its Predictable Place
The physical environment speaks to the nervous system. Chaos, clutter, and unpredictable organization signal threat. Consistency, order, and predictable placement signal safety. When everything has a place and is always in that place, children can navigate their environment without the cognitive and emotional load of searching, wondering, and adapting.
1
Identify the 10 Most-Used Items
Map out which items your child reaches for every day in their primary environment.
2
Assign Each a Fixed Location
Shoes → always this spot · Bag → always this hook · Water bottle → always this shelf · Preferred items → always this box.
3
Label with Pictures at Eye Height
Use photos or drawings, not just text. Clear containers so contents are visible without opening.
4
Never Reorganize Without Warning
Surprise reorganization is experienced as unpredictability, not improvement. Preview changes with photographs first. Allow practice time before relying on the new arrangement.

Materials 6 & 7: Safe Space + Comfort Objects
🏕️🧸 Step 6 of 9 — The Predictable Refuge That's Always There
When the world feels overwhelming, children need a place that is reliably, predictably safe. This predictable refuge reduces overall anxiety because the child knows: even when everything else is hard, this safe space exists.
Safe Space Setup
  • Enclosed enough to feel containing (canopy, tent, or curtain over corner)
  • Consistent soft seating: floor cushion or bean bag
  • Weighted blanket or lap pad for proprioceptive calming
  • Comfort items: same items, always there
  • Dim, consistent lighting + noise-reducing elements
  • Never used punitively — child-accessed refuge only
Cool-Down Protocol
  1. After high-demand periods: "You can go to your calm spot if you want."
  1. Child accesses safe space independently when needed
  1. No time limit — they exit when regulated
  1. After exit: brief warm reconnection before next demand
Comfort Objects — Portable Predictability
Comfort objects are portable predictability. The object is the same at home, at school, at grandma's, at the doctor. It carries the sensory familiarity of safety into environments that feel unsafe.
Allow comfort objects in all settings. Work with school to permit them. The predictability they carry across settings is part of their therapeutic value.

Session Abandonment Is Not Failure. It's Data.

Every family hits a wall. Here are the most common challenges in implementing C-300 tools — and the evidence-based responses to each. If something isn't working, the tool needs adjustment, not abandonment. "My child ignores the visual schedule completely." Are the images photos of actual activities (not generic symbols)? Start with 3 items maximum. Make it interactive: have child move/check off each item themselves. Involve child in creating it. "Transition warnings make it worse — they start panicking 10 minutes before." Expected at first — anxiety is being triggered earlier, which shows the tool is registering. Keep warnings shorter (2-min only initially). Practice transitions during low-stakes activities first. "My child won't go to the safe space — they follow me instead." The space needs more positive pairing before it can be a refuge. Spend pleasant time there first. Read stories there. Positive association must precede refuge use. "We can't maintain consistency across school and home." Start with home only. Establish reliability at home first, then share the approach with school. Same-setting reliability still builds trust — add settings once home is consistent. "I'm exhausted by maintaining all this consistency." You don't have to implement all 9. Start with 2. An imperfect consistent tool beats a perfect inconsistently-used tool every time. Caregiver sustainability is the foundation of child regulation. 📞 9100 181 181 — "Nothing is working." Our team will do a session analysis. Emergency: if child becomes severely distressed → stop, offer comfort, provide safe space access, note what happened, adjust before next attempt.

Your Questions, Answered by the Consortium
Why does my child ask the same questions even when I've answered?
The nervous system keeps checking because it cannot hold onto the feeling of safety — only the information. When information is externalized visually, repeated questioning reduces because the nervous system can verify safety without relying on memory.
How is using visual schedules different from "giving in" to rigidity?
Visual schedules are neurobiological accommodation, not reinforcement of rigidity. Flexibility is built on safety, not instead of it. Safety always precedes flexibility in therapeutic sequencing.
My child doesn't have an autism diagnosis. Are these materials still relevant?
Yes. These materials benefit any child with elevated predictability needs — anxiety disorders, sensory processing differences, trauma histories, high-sensitivity temperament, or developmental delays. A diagnosis is not required.
How long should I continue these supports?
Some children will use predictability supports throughout childhood and adolescence. This is legitimate neurobiological accommodation, not a developmental failure. The goal is functional life, not elimination of support needs.
Should I take away the comfort object at school?
No. Comfort objects are regulatory tools, not indulgences. Allow them in all settings. Work with the school to permit them. The predictability they carry across settings is part of their therapeutic value.
