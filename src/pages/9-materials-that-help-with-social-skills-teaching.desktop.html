9 Materials That Help With Social Skills Teaching
When your child wants friends but doesn't know how to make them — these tools make the invisible visible.
Social Communication · Episode 970

The Challenge
When Wanting Friends Isn't Enough
"My son is nine years old, and he desperately wants friends. I see it in his face when he watches other children playing together at the park. I hear it when he comes home from school and says nobody wanted to sit with him at lunch. He tries — really tries — but something always goes wrong. He stands too close. He talks only about dinosaurs even when the other child has clearly lost interest. He doesn't understand when kids are teasing him versus being friendly. He laughs at the wrong moments and misses jokes entirely. When he joins a game, he can't follow the unwritten rules that other children seem to know instinctively."
Birthday party invitations stopped coming in Class 2. Now in Class 4, he spends every recess alone or with the duty teacher. He asks me why nobody likes him. How do I even begin to answer that? How do I teach him something that should come naturally?
Understanding the Social Skills Gap
Social skills encompass the complex, largely unwritten rules that govern human interaction — knowing when to speak and when to listen, how close to stand, how to interpret facial expressions and tone of voice, how to join a group, maintain a conversation, share interests, handle disagreement, and build relationships over time.
For Neurotypical Children
Much of social learning is absorbed implicitly through observation and everyday experience — the hidden curriculum is absorbed without anyone teaching it.
For Children With Autism
The "hidden curriculum" of social interaction remains hidden. Social skills teaching makes the implicit explicit — breaking complex behaviors into learnable, practicable components.
Common Social Challenges
Children with autism and related conditions may experience a range of social difficulties. Recognizing the specific pattern helps target teaching most effectively.
1
Reading Faces & Body Language
Missing cues about boredom, irritation, or joy on others' faces and in their posture.
2
Tone & Sarcasm
Taking words literally; missing when humor, teasing, or frustration changes the meaning.
3
Conversational Reciprocity
Talking too much about personal interests without asking about the other person or reading signs of disinterest.
4
Joining & Entering Groups
Not knowing how to approach a group already in play, or leaving without understanding why rejection happened.
5
Perspective-Taking
Difficulty understanding that others have different thoughts, feelings, and knowledge — the foundation of empathy.
6
Friendship Intensity
Getting too close too fast, or withdrawing entirely — struggling with the gradual, reciprocal nature of friendship.
The Stakes: Why Social Skills Matter
The Emotional Cost of Isolation
Without social skills development, children become increasingly isolated as social demands grow with age. Loneliness affects mental health, self-esteem, and motivation. Missed social learning opportunities create widening gaps between a child and their peers.
The desire for connection without the skills to achieve it is profoundly painful — for the child and for every parent who watches it happen.

The good news: Social skills can be explicitly taught. What feels mysterious can be made visible, learnable, and practicable. Connection is possible.

Material 1 of 9
Social Stories for Social Situations
Social stories translate complex social situations into clear, understandable narratives. When your child doesn't understand why we take turns, what to do when someone says no, or how to join a game already in progress, a social story breaks it down — step by step, from the child's perspective.
How Social Stories Work
Each story describes a specific social situation, explains what happens and why, describes others' thoughts and feelings, and suggests appropriate responses. A story about joining a group might explain:
"Sometimes children are already playing together. I can watch for a moment to see what they're doing. I can walk closer and say, 'Can I play too?' They might say yes. If they say no, I can say, 'Maybe next time' and find something else to do."
The story makes the invisible visible, giving your child a script for situations that otherwise feel mysterious.
Key Insight
Social stories translate invisible social rules into visible, learnable steps — making the implicit explicit for children who need explicit teaching.
Price Range
₹300–1,500 (published) or Free (custom written by parent/therapist)
Safety Note
Social stories should be accurate and honest. Don't promise outcomes like "They will be my friend" — focus on what the child can control.
Writing Your Own Social Story: Step-by-Step
1
Identify the Situation
Choose a specific social challenge: joining play, handling rejection, sharing, turn-taking, greetings, conversations, dealing with teasing, or asking for help.
2
Gather Information
Observe the situation. Note what happens, who's involved, what the expectations are. Understand your child's current confusion and where the gap exists.
3
Write the Story
Use first person ("I"). Include descriptive sentences, perspective sentences ("Other children might feel..."), and directive sentences ("I can try to..."). Keep language simple and positive.
4
Format & Use
Create as a simple book (folded pages, stapled). Include photos or drawings. Laminate for durability. Read together regularly — before the situation occurs.

Materials needed: Paper or cardstock · Photos or drawings · Simple clear text · Laminating supplies (optional) · Binding (staples, rings, or folder)
Material 2 of 9
Emotion Recognition Cards and Games
Reading emotions is foundational to social interaction. If your child can't tell when someone is bored, annoyed, happy, or sad, they'll miss critical social cues at every turn. Emotion recognition materials use photos of real faces showing various emotions — teaching children to identify, name, and respond to what they see.
Building Emotional Literacy Progressively
Cards might show happy, sad, angry, scared, surprised, disgusted, confused, excited, bored, worried, and embarrassed expressions. Games build skills level by level — from matching emotions to predicting how actions might affect others' feelings.
With consistent practice, children learn to quickly read the emotional information on faces — information that guides every appropriate social response.
Creating Emotion Recognition Materials at Home
Photo Collection
  • Collect photos showing clear emotional expressions
  • Use family members (with permission) for familiarity
  • Include diverse ages, genders, and intensities
  • Start with basic emotions: happy, sad, angry, scared, surprised
  • Progress to subtle emotions: confused, bored, worried, embarrassed, disappointed
Game Ideas
  • Emotion matching game (find pairs)
  • "How are they feeling?" guessing game
  • Emotion charades
  • Story pause ("How is the character feeling now?")
  • Real-time practice ("Look at your brother's face — how is he feeling?")
Key Insight
Emotional literacy is learnable. With structured practice, children can learn to read the feelings that guide all social interaction.
Safety Note
Some children find certain emotions (especially anger or fear) distressing to view. Progress at your child's comfort level — never force exposure.
Price Range: ₹200–800
Material 3 of 9
Conversation Cue Cards and Scripts
Conversation is a complex dance — knowing when to speak and listen, how to stay on topic, when to change topics, how to show interest, and how to handle pauses. For children who struggle with spontaneous conversation, cue cards provide the structure that makes connection possible.
Cards might show conversation starters, follow-up questions, interest indicators, topic bridges, and conversation enders. Scripted conversations can be practiced until they become natural — not robotic recitation, but a framework the child eventually adapts and personalizes.
Cue Card Categories
1
Conversation Starters
  • "Hi, how are you?"
  • "What did you do today?"
  • "What do you like to do?"
2
Follow-Up Questions
  • "Tell me more."
  • "What happened next?"
  • "How did that feel?"
3
Interest Indicators
  • "That's cool!"
  • "Really?"
  • "That sounds fun."
4
Topic Bridges
  • "That reminds me of..."
  • "I also like..."
  • "Something similar happened to me..."
5
Conversation Enders
  • "I need to go now."
  • "Nice talking to you."
  • "Let's talk more another time."
Key Insight
Conversation structure can be taught. Pre-loaded phrases reduce cognitive load and free up mental energy to actually enjoy connection.
Price Range
₹100–400 or Free (homemade)
Safety Note
Scripts should sound natural, not robotic. Practice until delivery is smooth and flexible before expecting real-world use.
Conversation Mapping: The Visual Structure
Help your child see the pattern in every conversation. Once they understand that conversations have predictable structure — a beginning, a back-and-forth middle, and a close — the whole interaction becomes less overwhelming and more navigable.

Beyond scripts: As your child masters scripted responses, gradually introduce unexpected replies and how to handle them. The goal is flexible, genuine use — not memorization.
Material 4 of 9
Perspective-Taking Games and Activities
Theory of mind — understanding that others have different thoughts, feelings, knowledge, and perspectives — is often challenging for children with autism. Without this understanding, social interactions are deeply confusing. Why is my friend upset when I told the truth about her drawing? Why didn't she know about the surprise party when I knew?
Perspective-taking games explicitly teach that different people have different views, building the cognitive foundation for empathy and appropriate social responding.
Core Perspective-Taking Concepts
Visual Perspectives
Looking at objects from different angles — what can person A see versus person B? Simple physical perspective-taking is the earliest foundation.
Knowledge Differences
Person A knows something person B doesn't. Classic Sally-Anne false belief tasks teach that beliefs differ between people.
Desire Differences
Different people want different things. "I like chocolate, but my sister likes vanilla" — simple but powerful perspective grounding.
Thought Bubbles
Add thought bubbles to pictures showing what different characters might be thinking. Visualizing internal states makes abstract cognition concrete.
Key Insight
Perspective-taking is foundational. Understanding that others have different minds is the basis for all social navigation — and it can be systematically developed.
Price Range
₹300–1,200
Safety Note
Theory of mind develops at different rates. Don't rush or shame if your child struggles. These are complex cognitive skills that develop through patient, consistent teaching.
Perspective-Taking in Daily Life
Real-time practice during everyday moments is among the most powerful teaching available — no special materials required.
During Family Conversations
"What do you think your sister is feeling right now?" Point to facial expressions and ask your child to read them in the moment.
During Storytelling
Read books and pause: "What is this character thinking?" "Does the character know what we know?" "Why did they do that?" Comparing character perspectives builds social cognition naturally.
During Surprises or Secrets
"Dad doesn't know about the surprise — what should we not tell him?" Using familiar family scenarios creates meaning and motivation for perspective-taking practice.
Material 5 of 9
Social Skills Board Games
Learning social skills through games makes practice feel like play rather than therapy. Board games designed for social skills development present social scenarios and require players to demonstrate appropriate responses, identify emotions, practice conversation skills, or navigate social problem-solving — all while having fun.
The game format provides natural turn-taking, winning and losing practice, rule-following, and social interaction — all while explicitly targeting skill development. Children learn without feeling like they're in a lesson.
Creating Social Skills Games at Home
Adapt Any Existing Game
Add question cards to any board game. Players draw and answer before or after turns. Sample questions:
  • "Name one way to show you're listening."
  • "What would you do if someone said something mean?"
  • "Show us a surprised face."
  • "Practice greeting the person next to you."
Create Simple Games
  • Scenario Cards Game: Draw, read, and act out appropriate responses
  • Emotion Bingo: Match emotions to photos or scenarios
  • Social Skills Jenga: Prompts on blocks — practice skills as you pull
  • Role Play Spinner: Spin to land on situations to act out
  • Friendship Path: Complete social challenges to advance
Key Insight
Games create natural practice opportunities. Social skills learned through play are practiced with motivation and joy — the best conditions for real learning.
Price Range: ₹800–3,000
Safety Note
Competition can be challenging. Start with cooperative games if winning/losing is a significant difficulty — build tolerance for outcomes gradually.
Material 6 of 9
Video Modeling Resources
Seeing is understanding. Video modeling shows social skills in action, demonstrating exactly what appropriate behavior looks like. Children watch videos of peers or adults successfully navigating social situations — greeting someone, joining a game, having a conversation, handling conflict, asking for help.
They see the facial expressions, body language, words, and timing. Videos can be paused and discussed, replayed for mastery, and used as models for practice. Research strongly supports video modeling as an evidence-based practice for teaching social skills to children with autism.
Types of Video Modeling
Basic Video Modeling (BVM)
Others demonstrating the skill — peers, adults, or actors showing exactly what the target behavior looks like from start to finish.
Video Self-Modeling (VSM)
Child watches themselves successfully performing the skill (edited from practice footage). Seeing yourself succeed is powerfully confidence-building.
Point-of-View Modeling
Filmed from the child's perspective — showing what they would see. Ideal for spatial skills like personal space, entering groups, and turn-taking.
Key Insight
Seeing success creates success. Video models provide concrete, visual examples of abstract social skills — ideal for visual learners who struggle with verbal-only explanations.
Price Range
₹500–2,500 (commercial) or Free (homemade) — custom family videos are often the most effective due to familiarity and relevance.
Safety Note
Preview all videos first. Supervise online viewing carefully. Children may imitate both positive and negative modeled behaviors.
Creating Video Models at Home
1
Set Up the Scene
Use a smartphone — no special equipment needed. Choose realistic settings: playground, classroom, home. Keep each video short (30 seconds to 2 minutes).
2
Show Complete Skills
Film the skill from beginning to end, including a positive outcome. Script carefully but deliver naturally. Use siblings or peers as actors where possible.
3
Watch and Discuss Together
Watch multiple times. Ask: "What did she say? How did he look? What happened next?" Practice the skill immediately after viewing.
4
Fade the Support
Gradually reduce video watching as the skill becomes natural. The goal is independence — video models are scaffolding, not permanent support.
Material 7 of 9
Social Skills Curriculum Books
Comprehensive social skills curricula provide structured, sequential teaching across the full range of social abilities. For parents wondering "where do I even start?", curricula provide a roadmap. For therapists and teachers, they offer evidence-based frameworks that ensure comprehensive coverage rather than random skill selection.
The structure respects developmental building blocks — foundational skills first, complex skills built upon them — so no critical steps are skipped.
Popular Curricula Worth Knowing
Social Thinking® (Michelle Garcia Winner)
Focus on social cognition — helping children understand the "why" behind social expectations. Widely used by therapists and educators worldwide.
Skillstreaming Series
Structured skill-by-step approach available for preschool through adolescence. Breaks each skill into teachable components with lesson plans and practice activities.
PEERS® Curriculum
Program for the Education and Enrichment of Relational Skills. Teen-focused, evidence-based, developed at UCLA. Parent involvement is central.
The Hidden Curriculum
Understanding the unwritten social rules that schools and social groups assume children already know — invaluable for autism-specific teaching.
Superflex® / Social Thinking Superheroes
Engaging superhero framework for younger children (ages 4–9). Makes abstract social concepts concrete and motivating through character-based learning.
A Sample Curriculum Progression
Skills build on each other. Rushing to advanced skills before foundational ones are solid creates gaps. Follow the sequence, celebrate mastery, and move forward only when your child is consistently successful — not just occasionally.
Key Insight
Structure prevents gaps. Systematic curricula ensure comprehensive skill development rather than random, reactive teaching that leaves holes.
Price Range: ₹1,500–5,000
Safety Note
Curricula must be adapted to individual children. Not every skill applies to every child — good practitioners individualize within the framework.
Material 8 of 9
Role Play Props and Puppets
Practice makes permanent. Role play allows children to rehearse social situations in low-stakes settings before encountering them in real life. Props and puppets make role play engaging and reduce self-consciousness — it's the puppet asking to join the game, not the child directly.
Children can try different approaches, make mistakes safely, and build confidence before real-world application. The pretend space is where skills become natural.
Setting Up Role Play Practice
Puppet Options
  • Simple sock puppets (DIY with craft supplies)
  • Hand puppets representing different characters
  • Paper bag puppets
  • Finger puppets for portable practice
  • Stuffed animals as named characters
Scenario-Specific Props
  • Telephone for calling practice
  • Play food for restaurant/hosting scenarios
  • Ball for playground interaction
  • School supplies for classroom scenarios
  • Party supplies for birthday practice
Role Play Structure
01
Set the Scene
Describe the situation clearly before beginning.
02
Model First
Adult demonstrates with puppets if needed.
03
Child Practices
Let child attempt the scenario.
04
Provide Feedback
Specific, positive, constructive — celebrate effort.
05
Repeat with Variations
What if they said something different? Add complexity gradually.
Scenario Library for Role Play
Greetings
Greeting friends, adults, familiar vs. unfamiliar people — with appropriate eye contact and voice volume.
Joining a Game
Approaching a group already in play, asking to join, following their rules, and handling being told no.
Handling No
Responding when someone says no to a request — borrowing, playing, or asking for help.
Giving Compliments
Offering and receiving kind words gracefully — without minimizing or rejecting the compliment.
Managing Conflict
Handling disagreement or a mistake — using calm words, listening, and repairing the relationship.
Asking for Help
Identifying the right person, approaching appropriately, making a clear request — from familiar and unfamiliar adults.
Key Insight
Rehearsal builds confidence. Practicing in pretend prepares children for success in reality — and confidence in pretend transfers to courage in real life.
Price Range: ₹300–1,500
Safety Note
Role play should feel safe and fun, not stressful. If your child becomes anxious, simplify the scenario or take a break. The goal is confidence, not pressure.
Material 9 of 9
Social Skills Tracker and Reward System
Motivation and measurement drive progress. A tracking system shows your child — and you — which skills are improving and which need more practice. A reward system provides motivation for practicing skills that feel difficult or unnatural. Together, they create a sustainable practice system that celebrates effort and builds momentum.
Seeing growth builds confidence. Earning recognition for effort encourages continued practice even when skills are hard.

Untitled card

What to Track and How
What to Track
  • Target skills currently being practiced
  • Attempts (not just successes) — effort matters
  • Daily or weekly data
  • Specific, observable behaviors
Examples: greeted a peer, asked a question in conversation, handled disagreement calmly, joined a group activity.
Tracking Formats
  • Simple chart: Skills on one axis, days on the other — mark each success
  • Sticker chart: Visual and motivating for younger children
  • Star or point system: Each skill practice earns points toward rewards
  • Journal format: Brief notes on daily social interactions
  • App-based tracking: For older children or parents who prefer digital
Reward System Principles
Reward Effort, Not Just Success
Acknowledge attempts and improvement. Practicing hard things deserves recognition even when the outcome isn't perfect.
Use Meaningful Rewards
Stickers, points toward preferred activities, extra screen time, choice of family outing — whatever genuinely motivates your specific child.
Start Frequent, Then Fade
Begin with frequent rewards to build momentum. As skills become natural and intrinsically rewarding, gradually reduce external recognition.
Celebrate Patterns
Weekly progress reviews create positive reflection habits. "Look how far you've come!" is often the most powerful reward of all.
Key Insight
What gets measured improves. Tracking plus rewards creates sustainable motivation for challenging skill development — turning a difficult journey into a celebrated one.
Price Range: ₹100–500 or Free
Safety Note
Avoid making social interaction feel like constant evaluation. Track supportively, not judgmentally. Reward effort and growth — never shame setbacks.

Where to Start: Recommended Progression
Not all materials are created equal in terms of where to begin. This sequence builds skills in the order that creates the strongest foundation — each step supporting the next.
Step 1: Emotion Recognition Cards
Foundation of all social skills — reading feelings enables every appropriate response.
Step 2: Social Stories
Use emotional understanding to navigate specific situations — make the invisible visible.
Step 3: Perspective-Taking Games
Build the social cognition that makes empathy and appropriate responding possible.
Step 4: Conversation Cue Cards
Apply social understanding to real conversation — give your child the practical language for connection.
Step 5: Role Play Props
Practice in safe, low-stakes settings before bringing skills into the real world.
Step 6: Video Modeling
Add visual examples — show exactly what success looks like for visual learners.
Step 7: Social Skills Games
Make practice enjoyable — build motivation and generalize skills through play.
Step 8: Curriculum Book
Introduce structured progression to ensure no gaps in comprehensive skill development.
Step 9: Tracking System
Add measurement and motivation — sustain the practice that turns skills into habits.

Social Skills Can Be Taught
For many children with autism, the "hidden curriculum" of social interaction remains invisible until someone makes it explicit. These materials take abstract social concepts and make them concrete, learnable, and practicable.
For Parents
Your child's desire for connection is real. What they need are the tools to achieve it. With systematic teaching, structured practice, and patient support, children who once felt "not made for friends" can develop skills that lead to genuine relationships.
For Therapists & Educators
This isn't about making children pretend to be someone they're not. It's about giving them tools to express who they are in ways others can understand — and connect with. Explicit instruction is not a lesser alternative to natural learning; it is the appropriate intervention for children whose social learning pathways differ.
