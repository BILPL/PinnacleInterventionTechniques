You Are Among Millions of Families Navigating This Exact Challenge
Jargon speech — long streams of speech-like sounds with adult intonation but no recognizable words — is developmentally typical from 12–18 months. When it persists or dominates past 24 months without clear words emerging, it signals a mismatch between two developing systems: prosody (the melody of language, which your child has already mastered) and phonology + lexicon (the actual words, which need targeted support to emerge). This is not silence. This is not defiance. This is the vocal system searching for the word map.

Safety First — Read This Before Starting. Every Time.
🔴 Contraindications — Do Not Proceed If:
  • Child has an active ear infection or recent hearing test pending — jargon may have audiological etiology; assess hearing first
  • Child shows signs of motor speech disorder — inconsistent production, effortful groping for articulation, significant drooling changes — refer to SLP before home program
  • Child is in acute sensory distress (meltdown within past 60 minutes, highly elevated arousal, non-responsive to name)
  • Fever above 38°C or signs of illness
🟡 Material Safety — Check Before Each Session:
  • Choking hazards: All card and board materials must be A5 or larger — no small pieces for children under 3
  • Mirror safety: Use acrylic/unbreakable mirror only. No glass mirrors at child height
  • App screen time: Maximum 10 minutes per session. Always with adult present
  • Cause-effect toys: Check battery compartment security; small batteries are choking/poisoning hazards
🔴 Stop If You See These Signs (During Session):
  • Child begins covering ears or eyes consistently when materials are presented
  • Increase in jargon volume/distress (not decrease) as session continues
  • Child produces regression in previously acquired words
  • Physical distress signals: breath changes, colour changes, extreme crying that doesn't settle
  • Any sign of frustration escalating toward self-injurious behavior

The Right Space Makes the Words Come Easier
The therapeutic environment is not optional — it is part of the intervention. A well-structured space reduces competing stimuli, optimizes your child's arousal state, and makes every word model more effective.
Child Positioning
  • Seated on floor mat or low chair — not parent's lap (child needs to face you)
  • Both feet flat — stability supports focus
  • Eye level matched with parent — not looking up
Parent Positioning
  • Seated at child's eye level (on floor, on low stool)
  • Directly in front of child's face — not to the side
  • Face fully visible — your mouth is their visual model
  • Phone on silent, face down
Environment Checklist
  • TV off (auditory competition)
  • Room reasonably quiet
  • Materials in a bag or box — reveal one at a time (novelty maintains attention)
  • Natural lighting — not harsh overhead (calm arousal state)
  • 10-minute timer set (start short, build duration)
⏰ Best Session Times
  • After morning feed — alert, content, not yet tired
  • After afternoon nap — rested, regulated
  • Avoid: Immediately before sleep, during hunger/thirst, right after outdoor play

Body Language Guide
Face: Animated, smiling, at child's eye level. Voice: Slightly higher pitch, slower pace, clear consonants. Body: Leaning toward child, open posture, no hovering. Eyes: Looking at child's face, not the material.
Resistance Protocol

Step 2 of 6: The Engagement
Step 2 of 6
Time: 1–3 minutes
The Word Model. Clear. Slow. Once. Wait. This step contains the core SLP technique: Focused Stimulation — providing multiple clear, natural models of a target word in the same short window without demanding production.
You say: "[Material name]. It's [material name]. Look — [material name]!"

Example with ball: "Ball. It's a ball. Look — BALL!" (hold up, point to, tap the ball three times while saying the word)

Then: 🤫 Wait 5 full seconds. Look at child expectantly. Smile. Don't fill the silence.

🧠 Why waiting works: The 5-second pause creates a communication opportunity — a gap the child's prosodic system immediately "feels." Many children produce their first word approximation in the pause. Filling the pause eliminates this learning moment entirely.

Step 3 of 6: The Therapeutic Action
Step 3 of 6
Time: 3–8 minutes | The Active Ingredient
Multi-Material Word Cycling. Rotate through 3 of your 9 materials in one session (15–20 minutes maximum). Each material creates a different neural pathway to the SAME target word. This cross-modal reinforcement is the primary mechanism of jargon reduction.
Material 1 — Core Vocabulary Card (3 repetitions)
Hold up the "MORE" card → say "MORE" clearly → wait 5 seconds → if child vocalizes anything, ACTIVATE a reward → say "MORE! Yes, more!" → repeat 2× more times
Material 3 — Cause-Effect Toy (3 repetitions)
Hold ball run still → say "GO!" → wait → if any vocalization → release ball → "GO! Yes! GO!" → build excitement → repeat 3×
Material 8 — Song
"The wheels on the bus go ___..." → PAUSE → Wait for fill-in → If child attempts: "BUS! Yes! BUS!" → complete song → repeat chorus

Step 4 of 6: Repeat and Vary
Step 4 of 6
Time: 2–5 minutes
3 Good Repetitions > 10 Forced Ones. The brain consolidates a word faster when it hears it in multiple contexts — this is called distributed practice with contextual variation. Same word, different materials, different emotions, different positions.
Variation A — Same Word, Different Material
"Ball" with vocabulary card → "ball" with actual ball → "ball" in song ("Roll the ball to me") — same target, three distinct neural pathways
Variation B — Different Emotional Register
Excited "GO!" for the toy → quiet "go" during book → whispered "go" for suspense — prosodic variation + phonological consistency
Variation C — Different Positions
Word modeled while seated → while standing → during movement activity — motor context changes, word target constant

Step 5 of 6: Reinforce and Celebrate
Step 5 of 6
Timing: Within 3 seconds of each attempt
Celebrate the Attempt. Not Just the Success. Every approximation toward the target word is a correct response and deserves immediate, enthusiastic reinforcement.
For an approximation ("Buh" for "ball")
"YES! 'Buh'! BALL! You said ball! AMAZING!" — The reinforcement contains the corrective model, delivered with celebration.
For the exact word
"BALL! You said BALL! That is PERFECT!" — High-energy, immediate, specific praise.
For jargon (no approximation)
"I see you trying! Ball. BALL." — Warm acknowledgment + model — no negative reaction.
For silence
"Let's try — ball!" — Re-model with no pressure, forward momentum, and warmth.

Step 6 of 6
Time: 2–3 minutes | End Every Session With a Clear Signal
Abrupt session endings can dysregulate children who were engaged in a demanding (even pleasurable) activity. The cool-down is not optional — it is the final therapeutic action of every session.
2 Minutes Before Ending
"Two more times, then all done!" (Hold up 2 fingers.) Give the child a concrete, predictable warning.
1 Minute Before
"One more! Then — ALL DONE!" Use the "all done" sign simultaneously if you've been using signs throughout.
At Ending
"ALL DONE! Great work! You tried SO many words today." Specific, warm, celebratory closure.
Material Put-Away Ritual
"Let's put the cards away — card, card, card..." Child participates if able — builds closure routine and reduces resistance.
Announce the Next Activity
"Now we're going to have a snack / go outside / watch your show." Predictability = reduced resistance. Always announce what comes next.

Most Sessions Don't Go Perfectly. That's Normal. That's Data.
Troubleshooting is part of the protocol — not a sign that you're doing it wrong. Here are the 7 most common challenges and their precise solutions.
🔴 Child ran away after 2 minutes
Why: Session demand too high OR material not motivating enough today.
Fix: Shorten to 5-minute target. Switch to highest-preference material. Reduce word demand — pure modeling only, no waiting for response.
🔴 Jargon increased during session
Why: Child is communicating intensely — the session is activating their communication system. This is activation, not regression.
Fix: This is fine for weeks 1–4. Reduce session length and demand only if jargon increases with clear distress signs.
🔴 Child produced no word approximations at all
Why: Materials may not be motivating enough; child may not be in optimal arousal state; target words may be too distant from current ability.
Fix: Identify any item/activity that makes this child visibly excited. Start there. Accept vocalizations of ANY kind as success for first 2 weeks.
🟡 Child produces the same word for everything
Why: That word is well-consolidated — it's the child's "general purpose" word. Normal early vocabulary behavior.
Fix: Accept and expand: "Yes! Ball! And — car! Car!" Model the new word immediately after accepting theirs.
🟡 Child refuses the mirror
Why: Some children with sensory differences have difficulty with reflective surfaces or self-image.
Fix: Skip mirror for 2 weeks. Use video on phone instead. Reintroduce mirror gradually — start with their favorite toy in the mirror, not their face.
🟡 Sibling keeps interrupting
Fix: Sessions must have a dedicated 10-minute quiet window. If impossible — use sibling as communication partner: sibling says word, child imitates.
🟡 Progress seems to stop after initial gains
Why: Plateau is normal — word consolidation often precedes the next burst.
Fix: Return to highest-mastered words for 1 week (consolidation). Then introduce 1 new word while maintaining 3–4 consolidated ones.
💜 "Session abandonment is not failure. It is your child communicating their current capacity. That IS the data. Tomorrow is another session."

More Questions From Parents Like You
Q5: How many words should my child have at [age]?
WHO/ASHA developmental norms: 10 words by 12 months, 50+ words by 24 months, 200+ words by 36 months. Words include consistent approximations — if your child says "buh" consistently for ball, "ball" counts. If your child is significantly below these benchmarks and jargon is dominant, a speech-language evaluation is warranted. Book an assessment at your nearest Pinnacle centre or call 9100 181 181.
Q6: What if I'm doing it wrong?
The most common "wrong" approaches are: demanding correct pronunciation (don't), correcting the child's attempts (don't), or doing sessions when child is not in optimal state (reschedule instead). The cardinal rule: accept every vocalization, model the correct word, celebrate the attempt. You cannot harm your child's speech development by enthusiastically modeling words. You can only help.
Q7: Should I see an SLP before starting this at home?
Ideally yes — a baseline assessment helps identify the most functional word targets for YOUR child specifically, rules out motor speech disorders, and confirms hearing is not a factor. However, the B-187 protocol is designed for parent execution without requiring prior SLP contact. If you don't yet have SLP access, begin the protocol and call 9100 181 181 for guidance on next steps.
Q8: Will jargon ever stop completely?
In most children, as core vocabulary solidifies (30+ clear words), jargon naturally reduces and eventually resolves. In children with autism or more significant speech delays, jargon may persist longer but continues to reduce with consistent intervention. A small proportion of children may continue to use some jargon in excited states even as older children — this typically represents no functional impairment when they have clear core vocabulary.
