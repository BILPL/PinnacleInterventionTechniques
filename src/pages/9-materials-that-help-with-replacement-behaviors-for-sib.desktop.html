What's Happening in Your Child's Brain
Your child's brain has learned that a specific behavior — hitting, biting, scratching — reliably produces a result that their nervous system desperately needs. This is not a moral failure. It is operant learning at its most fundamental. The SIB-brain loop: unmet need → dysregulation → SIB → relief → reinforcement → repetition.
Sensory / Automatic
The nervous system seeks intense proprioceptive input — deep pressure, impact feedback, the organizing stimulation that only high-intensity sensation provides. The hitting works neurologically. It produces what the brain is missing. Our solution: match the intensity through safer input.
Escape / Avoidance
When demands are overwhelming — too much noise, too many instructions, too much sensory input — SIB creates an instant exit. Caregivers back off. Demands disappear. The child has learned: this gets me out. Our solution: teach them how to ask for the exit.
Attention / Connection
The child has learned that SIB produces the most intense, immediate, focused caregiver attention available. This is not manipulation — it is a profound unmet connection need finding its loudest expression. Our solution: flood the day with connection that doesn't require crisis.
Tangible / Access
SIB produces access to desired items or activities — a preferred toy returned, food provided, an activity restored. The behavior works. Our solution: teach appropriate requesting that works just as reliably.

Key Clinical Insight: The behavior is functional. It works. You cannot simply stop it without giving the nervous system another way to get what it needs. Suppression without replacement fails — always.

Where This Sits in Development
SIB is not a phase children grow out of. It's a communication system they grow through — when given better alternatives. The window for intervention is always open, but the approach must match the child's developmental stage. Understanding where your child sits on this timeline determines which replacement behaviors will work best.
Age 2–4
Head-banging and biting peak. Communication frustration highest. Emphasis on environmental modification, communication tools, sensory alternatives. High structure essential.
Age 4–6
SIB is now a learned behavior. Multiple functions may emerge. Structured replacement behavior training becomes possible. Multiple alternatives for different contexts.
Age 6–9
Behavioral complexity increases. Mixed triggers and functions. Replacement behaviors fully teachable across home, school, and community settings.
Age 9–12
Self-awareness may emerge. Child can recognize their own patterns and participate in understanding their needs. Self-monitoring skills begin to develop alongside replacement behaviors.

Comorbidity Awareness: SIB is significantly more prevalent with limited or no spoken communication (highest risk), sensory processing differences, intellectual disability, genetic syndromes (Lesch-Nyhan, Cornelia de Lange, Rett), and undiagnosed pain conditions. Each co-occurrence changes which replacement behaviors will work — and why function-based assessment matters before intervention.

The Technique: Replacement Behavior Training for SIB
🔄 Domain D — Behavioral Safety
Age 2–12
Replacement Behavior Training is the evidence-based practice of identifying why a child engages in self-injurious behavior — what need it serves, what function it fulfills — and then systematically teaching and reinforcing alternative behaviors that meet that same need through a safer channel.
The core principle: you cannot simply stop a behavior that works. You must offer something that works equally well or better, through a path that doesn't cause harm. The replacement behavior must be functionally equivalent — it must produce the same result the SIB was producing.

Who Uses This Technique — It Belongs to the Whole Child
This technique doesn't belong to one therapy. The brain doesn't organize itself by therapy type — a child hitting their head may be doing so because of a sensory need (OT domain), an inability to communicate pain (SLP domain), a learning history established by accidental reinforcement (ABA domain), or an undiagnosed ear infection (medical domain) — or all four simultaneously.
🟦 ABA / BCBA — Primary Lead
Conducts Functional Behavior Assessment. Designs replacement behavior plan. Trains all caregivers. Monitors data. Adjusts reinforcement schedules across all environments.
🟩 OT — Sensory Layer
Identifies sensory functions of SIB. Recommends intensity-matched alternatives. Designs sensory diet to proactively reduce triggers before they escalate.
🟨 SLP — Communication Layer
Designs AAC systems and FCT protocols. Teaches functional communication replacements. Trains parents in honoring all communication attempts — however they emerge.
🟥 SpEd — Educational Layer
Implements replacement behavior plan in school. Ensures consistency across all educational settings. Coordinates data collection with the ABA team.
🟪 NeuroDev Pediatrics — Medical Layer
Rules out pain and medical causes. Investigates neurological factors. Coordinates medical management and provides prognosis guidance for the team.
